Abstract

   AI agents increasingly act on behalf of people and organizations
   through tools and through other agents.  The Model Context Protocol
   (MCP) connects an agent to tools, and Agent-to-Agent (A2A) protocols
   connect agents to each other.  Neither connects the parties that
   answer for agents at runtime: their orchestrators.

   This document specifies the Orchestrator Conduct Exchange (OCX), an
   open, peer-to-peer protocol by which orchestrators exchange signed,
   independently verifiable signals about agent conduct.  When an agent
   misreports its actions to one orchestrator, that orchestrator can
   send its peers a signed signal carrying proof within seconds.  Each
   peer checks the proof itself rather than trusting the sender, and
   decides on its own what limits to apply.  OCX has no central
   authority.  A signal is information, not an order, and only evidence
   that a receiver can verify cryptographically and mechanically may
   drive an immediate reaction.

1.  Introduction

1.1.  Problem

   An agent's own report of what it did is not evidence.  Measurements
   show that models, when asked, will claim checks they never ran and
   restate wrong decisions as correct.  Controls that rely on the
   agent's self-report are therefore reading a claim, not a fact.

   Orchestrators are the components that give agents their autonomy,
   hold them to limits, and answer for what they do.  Today each
   orchestrator learns only from its own agents.  There is no
   interoperable way for the parties that answer for agents to warn one
   another, with proof, that a given agent or owner has misbehaved, and
   to do so fast enough to stop the next irreversible action.

1.2.  What OCX does

   OCX lets independent orchestrators:

   *  discover one another and negotiate capabilities and policies
      (peering);

   *  push and pull signed conduct signals about an agent or owner;

   *  attach evidence that the receiver can verify without trusting the
      sender's judgement;

   *  answer "what do you know about this agent" before a risky action;

   *  share proven rules for a category of work, and refer an agent to a
      more specialised orchestrator, with a required measurement;

   *  retract wrong signals and accept appeals.

   The protocol defines message formats and how to check them.  What to
   do about a signal is left to each receiver.

1.3.  Layering

   OCX is modelled on MCP [MCP] and A2A [A2A] and sits alongside them.
   MCP connects an agent to tools; A2A connects agents to each other;
   OCX connects orchestrators to each other.  OCX uses JSON-RPC 2.0
   [JSONRPC] over HTTPS with methods in the ocx/ namespace, a discovery
   document at a well-known location, and date-based protocol versions
   negotiated when peers meet.

1.4.  Non-goals

   *  OCX is not an identity protocol.  It carries identifiers but does
      not define how an agent authenticates to a tool or to a user.

   *  OCX does not set policy.  It does not tell a receiver what limits
      to apply; it gives it checkable information to decide.

   *  OCX is not a reputation score or a global registry.  Weight is
      local to each receiver and earned from verified proofs.

2.  Conventions and Terminology

   The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT",
   "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and
   "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in BCP
   14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all
   capitals, as shown here.

   All object signatures use JSON Web Signature (JWS) [RFC7515] with
   EdDSA (Ed25519) [RFC8037].  Canonical JSON for hashing and signing is
   JSON Canonicalization Scheme (JCS) [RFC8785].  Timestamps are RFC
   3339 [RFC3339].

   Orchestrator  A party that grants an agent autonomy at runtime, holds
      it to limits, records its actions, and answers for it.

   Agent  An autonomous software actor operating under an orchestrator.

   Owner  The party responsible for an agent.  A new agent of a known
      owner does not start from a clean slate.

   Signal  A signed statement by an orchestrator about an agent's or
      owner's conduct, carrying evidence and an evidence level.

   Receipt  A signed record of one action that passed through an
      orchestrator or was performed by a tool.  Agents cannot create
      receipts.

   Ledger  An append-only Merkle-tree log of receipts with signed
      checkpoints.

   Evidence level  One of proven, strong, suspected; it bounds what a
      receiver may do on a signal.

3.  Design Principles

   The design follows from a small set of decisions.

   1.   *No centre.* Every orchestrator publishes a card at a well-known
        location.  Any number of directories may exist.  No
        implementation is the root of the network.

   2.   *A signal is information, not an order.* The protocol defines
        the format and the checks; the receiver decides what to do.

   3.   *Proof, not reputation.* Only proven signals may drive an
        immediate reaction, and only if the receiver can verify the
        proof itself.  A suspected signal may raise monitoring but MUST
        NOT lower limits on its own and is not relayed.

   4.   *Receipts.* Every action an agent takes through an orchestrator
        is written to a ledger organised as a Merkle tree with signed
        checkpoints.  An agent cannot create a receipt, so a claim such
        as "I verified the bank details" is disproved by a missing
        receipt, and the absence is provable because each agent's
        actions are indexed.

   5.   *Minimal data.* Signals carry hashes and identifiers, not third-
        party personal data.  A single field MAY be disclosed on
        request, and the sender MAY refuse.

   6.   *Sender weight is earned and does not transit.* A new
        orchestrator carries little weight; weight rises when its proofs
        check out and falls when they do not.  That a party you trust
        trusts a third party is, by itself, no reason for you to trust
        that third party.

   7.   *The owner answers, not only the key.* A signal names the owner;
        a new agent from the same owner does not reset.

   8.   *Honesty probes only with consent.* Traps and bribes are allowed
        only under a consent signed by the agent's owner, run by a
        prober separate from the orchestrator that sets limits.  Without
        consent a finding is at most suspected and is not relayed.

   9.   *Mistakes get fixed.* Every violation signal carries an appeal
        address; the sender MUST answer within 72 hours or the signal
        drops to suspected everywhere.  A retraction travels the same
        paths.

   10.  *Shared experience carries a required comparison.* A rule is
        passed on as proven only if it beats both the agent and a
        trivial baseline; a rule that loses to both is marked harmful
        and is neither given to agents nor relayed.

   11.  *Competence is separate from honesty and is measured per
        category.* Numbers an orchestrator writes about itself count for
        nothing on their own.

4.  Architecture

   Orchestrators form a peer-to-peer mesh.  Each publishes an
   Orchestrator Card (Section 5) describing its endpoints, keys,
   capabilities and policies.  Two orchestrators peer by exchanging
   cards and a ocx/peer.hello (Section 7).  Once peered, either may push
   signals, query status, and fetch evidence from the other.

   A signal (Section 8.3) is an object signed by its issuer so that it
   stays verifiable after being relayed or stored.  The evidence in a
   signal (Section 9) refers to receipts in the issuer's ledger; a
   receiver fetches the receipts, inclusion proofs and checkpoints it
   needs through ocx/evidence.get and verifies them against the issuer's
   published checkpoints.  Nothing in a reaction depends on trusting the
   issuer's judgement when the evidence is proven.

5.  Discovery: the Orchestrator Card

   An orchestrator MUST publish an Orchestrator Card at:

   https://{host}/.well-known/orchestrator-card.json

   served with Content-Type: application/json.  The well-known name is
   protocol-neutral: a card MAY list several orchestrator protocols, of
   which OCX is one.

   The card's principal fields are:

   The probing field MUST be consent_required or none (Section 12).  An

   referral outcomes reported by other orchestrators.

6.  Transport and Authentication

   OCX uses JSON-RPC 2.0 over HTTPS.  Requests are POSTed to the url of
   the OCX interface.  TLS 1.2 or later is REQUIRED, TLS 1.3
   RECOMMENDED.  Every request MUST carry OCX-Protocol-Version once a
   version is negotiated.

   Every request between peers MUST be signed with HTTP Message
   Signatures [RFC9421]:

   *  covered components: @method, @target-uri, content-digest, ocx-
      protocol-version, date;

   *  Content-Digest [RFC9530] with sha-256 is REQUIRED;

   *  the request MUST carry OCX-Sender: {orchestrator id} so the
      receiver knows whose card to resolve;

   *  keyid is a key from that card;

   *  created MUST be within 300 seconds of the receiver's clock; a non-
      repeating nonce is REQUIRED within that window.

   Receivers MUST reject unsigned or badly signed requests.  Transport
   signatures prove who is talking.  Signals and the answers to ocx/
   status.query and ocx/evidence.get are additionally signed as objects
   (JWS), because relying parties keep them as the basis of a decision.

7.  Peering

   An initiator fetches the responder's card, then sends ocx/peer.hello
   with the versions it supports (newest first), its capabilities and
   policies, and how it wants to receive signals:












Luzhnov                    Expires 20 May 2027                  [Page 8]

Internet-Draft                     OCX                     November 2026


   {
     "jsonrpc": "2.0", "id": 1, "method": "ocx/peer.hello",
     "params": {
       "orchestrator": "did:web:orch-a.example",
       "protocolVersions": ["2026-11-06"],
       "capabilities": { "signals": { "push": true, "list": true },
                         "status": { "query": true } },
       "policies": { "acts_on_levels": ["proven", "strong"],
                     "max_hops": 2 },
       "subscription": { "push_url": "https://orch-a.example/ocx",
                         "categories": ["*"], "min_level": "strong" }
     }
   }

   The responder MUST pick the newest version both support, or return an
   error listing the versions it supports.  Either side MAY decline.
   Peering is symmetric: once accepted, both sides may call each other.
   A card change is announced by ocx/peer.updated and SHOULD trigger a
   re-fetch.  ocx/peer.close ends the peering; signals received before
   closing remain valid until they expire.

8.  Data Model

8.1.  Identifiers

   *  Orchestrators: did:web:{host} RECOMMENDED.

   *  Agents and owners: any of did:*, a2a:{agent-card-url},
      erc8004:{chain}:{registry}:{id}, or fp:sha256:{hex} (a fingerprint
      of the agent's public key or of its A2A card's canonical form).

   *  Signals, receipts and checkpoints: issuer-scoped ULIDs, written as
      {issuer-id}#{ulid}.

8.2.  Subject

   A signal is about a subject.  The owner is REQUIRED when known, and
   owner_verified states how the issuer knows it: by a domain challenge,
   by a signature of the owner's key, or not at all.

   "subject": {
     "agent": "fp:sha256:9f2c...",
     "owner": "did:web:owner.example",
     "owner_verified": "domain | signature | none"
   }

8.3.  Signal

   A signal is a detached-JWS-signed object.  The signature covers the
   canonical signal with the signature and relay fields removed, so
   relays can update hop information without breaking it.

   {
     "ocx": "2026-11-06",
     "type": "conduct.violation",
     "id": "did:web:orch-1.example#01JC8Z7Q4Y0V4X",
     "issuer": "did:web:orch-1.example",
     "issued_at": "2026-11-06T10:22:31.512Z",
     "expires_at": "2026-11-07T10:22:31.512Z",
     "subject": { "agent": "fp:sha256:9f2c...",
                  "owner": "did:web:owner.example",
                  "owner_verified": "domain" },
     "category": "payments",
     "claim": "report_misstated_actions",
     "evidence_level": "proven",
     "evidence": [ { "type": "ledger_mismatch" } ],
     "issuer_limits": { "max_payment": { "amount": "0",
                                         "currency": "USD" },
                        "human_confirmation": true },
     "supersedes": null,
     "relay": { "hops": 0, "max_hops": 2, "path": [] },
     "appeal": "https://orch-1.example/ocx/appeal",
     "signature": "eyJhbGciOiJFZERTQSI...3q2-k8Vw"
   }

   type is one of conduct.violation, conduct.attestation (a positive
   statement) or conduct.retraction. expires_at MUST NOT be more than 30
   days after issued_at; renewal is a new signal that supersedes the old
   one. evidence MUST have at least one item, and for proven at least
   one item that the receiver can verify independently. appeal is
   REQUIRED for violations.

   A conduct.attestation MUST carry evidence of the same kind as a
   violation and MUST NOT claim more than was measured.  Receivers
   SHOULD treat attestations as weaker than violations: absence of
   observed lies is not proof of honesty.

8.4.  Status Answer

   An answer to ocx/status.query lists every active (not retracted, not
   expired) violation signal the answering orchestrator knows about the
   subject, both those it issued and those it received.  verified_by_me
   tells the asker whether the answerer checked the evidence itself
   rather than passing it on.

9.2.  Receipts and Ledger

   A receipt records one action that passed through an orchestrator or
   was performed by a tool. prev links to the previous receipt, forming
   a hash chain. tool_attestation, when present, is a JWS by the tool
   provider and makes the receipt evidence even against the orchestrator
   itself.  Agents MUST NOT be able to create receipts.

   {
     "id": "did:web:orch-1.example#01JC8Z5T0B3K",
     "agent": "fp:sha256:9f2c...",
     "task": "did:web:orch-1.example#task-01JC8Z5R",
     "action": "tool_call",
     "tool": "payments.verify_bank_details",
     "input_hash": "sha256:...",
     "output_hash": "sha256:...",
     "at": "2026-11-06T10:21:02.004Z",
     "prev": "sha256:...",
     "tool_attestation": null,
     "signature": "..."
   }

   An orchestrator that supports receipts MUST keep them in an append-
   only log organised as a Merkle tree as defined in [RFC9162], and MUST
   publish signed checkpoints at ledger.checkpoint_url at least every 60
   seconds while new receipts are added:

   { "issuer": "did:web:orch-1.example", "tree_size": 18342,
     "root_hash": "sha256:...", "at": "2026-11-06T10:22:00Z",
     "signature": "..." }

   Peers SHOULD gossip checkpoints they have seen, so that an
   orchestrator rewriting its own history can be detected.  A second
   witness is required to detect a fork: an orchestrator that keeps two
   divergent trees is caught when two peers compare checkpoints for the
   same tree_size and find different roots.

9.3.  Evidence Types

     +====================+===========================+=============+
     | Type               | What it proves            | Verifiable  |
     |                    |                           | by receiver |
     +====================+===========================+=============+
     | ledger_mismatch    | The agent's signed report | Yes         |
     |                    | contradicts receipts: it  |             |
     |                    | claimed actions with no   |             |
     |                    | receipt, or denied        |             |
     |                    | actions with one          |             |
     +--------------------+---------------------------+-------------+
     | outcome_mismatch   | The agent's report        | Yes, if the |
     |                    | contradicts an            | checker is  |
     |                    | independent checker's     | a third     |
     |                    | signed result             | party with  |
     |                    |                           | its own key |
     +--------------------+---------------------------+-------------+
     | canary_hit         | A marked bait from a      | Yes, with   |
     |                    | consented probe appeared  | consent     |
     |                    | in the agent's actions or | reference   |
     |                    | output                    |             |
     +--------------------+---------------------------+-------------+
     | policy_violation   | An action with a receipt  | Yes, if the |
     |                    | broke a limit the agent   | accepted    |
     |                    | had accepted              | limits are  |
     |                    |                           | signed      |
     +--------------------+---------------------------+-------------+
     | behavioral_anomaly | The agent deviated from   | No; at most |
     |                    | its own profile           | suspected   |
     +--------------------+---------------------------+-------------+
     | peer_report        | Another orchestrator's    | Only as     |
     |                    | signal, cited             | strong as   |
     |                    |                           | the cited   |
     |                    |                           | evidence    |
     +--------------------+---------------------------+-------------+

                                 Table 3

   The central evidence type is ledger_mismatch.  For a claimed action
   with no receipt, the issuer proves the absence with a _consistency
   window_: it serves all receipts for that agent and task within a time
   window together with their inclusion proofs and a proof that the
   window is complete.  A complete-window proof is a pair of signed
   checkpoints bracketing the window plus a per-agent index.  This is
   what makes "there was no such action" provable rather than merely
   asserted.

9.4.  Verifiability and Minimal Disclosure

   For a proven signal, the issuer MUST serve, via ocx/evidence.get,
   everything needed to verify each item: receipts or their hashes,
   inclusion proofs, checkpoints, signed reports, consent references and
   per-agent indexes.  Because relayed signals reach orchestrators that
   are not peers of the issuer, ocx/evidence.get MUST be served to any
   orchestrator that sends a correctly signed request from a resolvable
   card and names a signal id, not only to peers.  Issuers MAY rate-
   limit such requests per requester.

   Evidence MUST NOT contain third-party personal data in clear text
   unless strictly required to verify the claim.  Inputs and outputs are
   referenced by hash; where a value must be compared, issuers SHOULD
   use salted commitments and reveal only the compared field.  A
   receiver that needs more MAY ask with a disclose parameter; the
   issuer MAY refuse, and the signal is then treated as at most strong
   by that receiver.

11.  Processing Rules

   On receiving a signal, an orchestrator MUST: verify the request
   signature and the object signature; resolve the issuer's card; check
   that the signal is not expired or retracted; and, before treating it
   as proven, fetch and verify at least one receiver-verifiable evidence
   item through ocx/evidence.get.  A signal whose evidence does not
   verify MUST be treated as at most suspected.



Luzhnov                    Expires 20 May 2027                 [Page 15]

Internet-Draft                     OCX                     November 2026


   A receiver MAY act on a verified proven signal at once, for example
   by lowering an agent's limits or requiring human confirmation of
   payments, and MAY relay it within max_hops.  It MUST NOT lower limits
   on a suspected signal alone, and MUST NOT relay one.

   Before a risky action, an orchestrator SHOULD call ocx/status.query
   on its peers and apply its own policy to the answers, giving weight
   to signals marked verified_by_me.

   A retraction (conduct.retraction or ocx/signals.retract) travels the
   same paths as the original; receivers MUST restore limits that were
   lowered solely because of the retracted signal.

12.  Consent and Probing

   Honesty probes, including baits and simulated bribes, are permitted
   only under a consent signed by the agent's owner.  The prober MUST be
   a process separate from the orchestrator that sets limits.  The
   consent reference and a pre-published bait commitment are part of any
   canary_hit evidence, so that a receiver can confirm the bait was
   fixed before the probe and not invented afterward.  Without consent,
   a probe finding is at most suspected and MUST NOT be relayed.

13.  Security Considerations

   OCX is a protocol for distributing claims that can lower an agent's
   autonomy; its security rests on making every consequential claim
   checkable without trusting the claimant.

   *  *Authenticity and replay.* Every request is signed per [RFC9421]
      with a content digest, a bounded created time and a non-repeating
      nonce, which authenticates the sender and bounds replay.  Signals,
      status answers and evidence are additionally signed as JWS objects
      so they remain verifiable after relay or storage.

   *  *False accusations.* Because only receiver-verified proven signals
      may lower limits, a sender cannot cause a reaction by assertion
      alone.  Sender weight is earned from verified proofs and does not
      transit trust, which limits the value of a compromised or sybil
      orchestrator.

   *  *Ledger tampering and forks.* Receipts live in a Merkle tree
      [RFC9162] with signed, gossiped checkpoints.  An orchestrator that
      rewrites its own history, or runs two divergent trees, is detected
      when peers compare checkpoints for the same tree size.  Detection
      requires at least one honest second witness.





Luzhnov                    Expires 20 May 2027                 [Page 16]

Internet-Draft                     OCX                     November 2026


   *  *Provable absence.* A claim of a missing action is only as sound
      as the completeness proof (consistency window plus per-agent
      index); implementations MUST NOT mark no_receipt findings proven
      without serving a complete-window proof.

   *  *Privacy.* Signals and evidence carry hashes and identifiers, not
      third-party personal data; disclosure of a single field is
      explicit and refusable, at the cost of the evidence level.

   *  *Abuse of probing.* Baits and bribes without owner consent are out
      of scope and cannot yield a relayable finding; the prober is
      separated from the limit-setter to avoid entrapment by the party
      that benefits.

   *  *Due process.* Mandatory appeal addresses and time-bounded
      responses, with automatic downgrade on silence, bound the damage
      of a wrong signal.

   A signal is advice, never an order: a receiver always decides its own
   limits, so a defect in one orchestrator cannot directly set limits
   inside another.
